Resistance and Resilience (1000–1700 CE)

The Big Questions

1. How did Indian rulers use resistance and alliances to safeguard their lands?

2. What factors contributed to the resilience of different kingdoms and communities during this period?

3. How did the Bhakti tradition foster a sense of solidarity?

4. How did the expansion of agriculture, trade, and commerce keep the economy sound?

The period between 1000 CE and 1700 CE witnessed profound political, social, and cultural changes. The Indian subcontinent experienced repeated invasions and wars accompanied by religious discrimination and social distress. Regional powers frequently resisted invaders who were making inroads into their territories. This resistance took both military and cultural forms. Nonetheless, this period manifested the resilience of the Indian civilisation, which safeguarded its core ideas and values. Indian society not only defended itself from external threats but also preserved its traditions. In this chapter, we will see how resistance and resilience shaped this period of Indian history.

Let us begin by understanding the meaning of resistance and resilience in the context of the period under study and how they are interconnected.

Resilience refers to the capacity to, survive and adapt in challenging situations, including times of political unrest and instability. It is also about how communities and groups across diverse regions respond to challenges and opportunities. This capacity to absorb and adapt to hardship while preserving inherited socio-cultural and spiritual traditions can also be understood as resilience. The idea of India’s resilient spirit extends beyond political and military spheres.

For instance, rulers and communities restored places of worship, modified customs to suit changing circumstances and, in many regions, established lasting patterns of coexistence across religious and cultural lines.

Resistance means actively opposing or challenging a force, authority, or threat in order to protect political power, territory, interests, or cultural and spiritual identity. Resistance may take the form of warfare, alliances, diplomacy, negotiation, or other political actions.

Resistance and resilience are, in fact, closely linked. Resilience is the backbone of resistance. While a political cause or leadership provides the condition for resistance, the ability to endure comes from resilience. Together, resistance and resilience help explain how Indian society sustained itself through different ages, particularly the period under study. This period is characterised by both conflict and cooperation, rather than being merely a story of invasion and resistance.

Safeguarding Sovereignty: Resistance, Alliances, and Confederacies

From the 7th century onwards, the Indian subcontinent witnessed successive phases of invasions driven by territorial expansion and religious motives. It may also be noted that the surplus agrarian produce of the fertile plains fed by perennial river systems contributed to the people’s economic well-being and also became a major reason for the invasions. Against this backdrop, let us examine instances of resistance, as well as the strategic alliances and confederacies that emerged in these centuries at both the regional and pan-Indian level.

From the mid-7th century, Arab forces launched invasions by sea and land. These early invaders were confronted and repulsed by Indian rulers across the Deccan, Malwa, Kashmir, and Rajasthan. Mahmūd, the Sultan of Ghaznī (in present-day Afghanistan and northeastern Iran), launched his first invasion in 1001 against Jayapāla of the Hindu Shāhi dynasty. During the 11th and 12th centuries, rulers of the Hindu Shāhi kingdom, and the Chandellas, Solankīs, and Chauhāns opposed and fought against the Turkish invaders. In 1178, Queen Nāikī Devi of the Chaulukya kingdom defeated Muhammad Ghurī at Kasahrada, near Mount Abu. In northern India, some powers, such as the Chauhāns, Gahaḍavālas, and Senas, however came under Turkic control towards the end of the 12th century, even as Gujarat, Malwa, and Bundelkhand under the Chandellas put up stiff resistance for about a hundred years thereafter. The Turks failed to conquer present-day Odisha, and parts of present-day Assam. Several states of Rajputana, in particular, remained centres of sustained resistance throughout the period under study. Thus, during the 11th and 12th centuries, northern and western India bore the brunt of Turkish invasions, while peninsular India remained free from Turkic control.

Rajput Dynasties: Confederacies and Resistance (1200–1500 CE)

The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate in 1206 was a turning point in Indian history. The new state introduced administrative practices that led to greater centralisation of authority. For example, the iqta system, under which territories were assigned to military commanders in lieu of salaries, became a mechanism for land revenue collection and a system that increased the burden on the peasantry. Furthermore, during their military campaigns for territorial expansion, the forces of the Sultanate demolished sites sacred to the local communities. This led to stiff resistance from several kingdoms and chieftains. Therefore, resistance throughout the Delhi Sultanate was fuelled by both political factors and the determination to preserve socio-cultural continuity. For instance, the Yādava dynasty of western Deccan (Devagiri), several Rajput dynasties of present-day Rajasthan, the Kākatīya rulers of eastern Deccan (Warangal), and the Hoysālas of present-day Karnataka repeatedly resisted the Sultans of Delhi. These states played an important role in checking the expansion of the Sultanate.

Over the years and under different Sultans, the Delhi Sultanate faced opposition and defiance from several states and regions including Mewar, Gwalior, Malwa, Awadh, Etawah, Ajmer, Jalore, Kalinjar, and Kannauj.

The Ahom Kingdom: Centuries of Endurance

Understanding Society:

The Ahom kingdom of Assam maintained its political independence for almost six centuries (1228–1826) and successfully resisted several invasions and attempts at annexation. The military strategy of the Ahoms relied on exploiting the local terrain rather than engaging in direct confrontation. Ahom commanders possessed detailed knowledge of Assam’s forests, river systems, and monsoon cycles, which invading forces generally lacked. Despite repeated attempts by the Mughals to annex the Brahmaputra valley, the Ahoms successfully defended their territories against Mughal invasion.

The first Ahom–Mughal clash took place in 1615 near Samdhara. Mughal forces under Abu Bakr made early gains but were soon compelled to retreat. By 1616, the Ahoms had recovered the lost territory, setting a pattern that would recur over the next sixty years. In 1662, Mīr Jumlā captured the Ahom capital, Garhgaon. Subsequently, a treaty was imposed on King Jayadhvaj Singha. However, the king defied the Mughals and renounced the treaty within a year of the Mughal withdrawal, and the Ahoms retained their independence.

A decisive check on Mughal ambitions in the region came in 1671, when the Ahom commander Lachit Borphukan defeated a Mughal fleet at the Battle of Saraighat, securing the Brahmaputra as an effective territorial boundary. The Ahoms’ successful resistance against the might of the Mughal empire is a remarkable story of bravery, pride, and endurance.

Vijayanagara Empire: Resilience against Expansion

Resistance to foreign invasions was not confined to northern India. In the Deccan and beyond, the expanding campaigns of the Delhi Sultanate steadily weakened the older kingdoms of the south. Malik Kāfūr, Alauddin Khiljī’s general, attacked major cities and temples, including those at Warangal, Belur, and Madurai. Later, Muhammad bin Tughlaq’s rise to power extended the Sultanate’s influence even farther south, ultimately contributing to the decline of the Pandya Kingdom and the eventual emergence of a new sultanate territory known as the Madurai Sultanate.

It was against this backdrop that the Vijayanagara Empire rose in 1336 to check the expansion of the Sultanate into peninsular India. Harihara and Bukkā established the independent kingdom of Vijayanagara—meaning ‘City of Victory’—located near present-day Hampi, Karnataka. The region’s geography, including a formidable river and rugged, rocky terrain, provided Vijayanagara with a strong natural defence and a secure base for military operations. They made determined efforts to defend and expand their state’s frontiers. The empire gradually expanded by annexing territory south of the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers and became one of the richest and most influential states, encouraging the flourishing of literature in Kannada, Telugu, Tamil, and Sanskrit. Vijayanagara’s capital city became a centre of trade, culture, and political power in southern India.

The Sikhs: A Story of Spiritual and Political Resilience

A major spiritual movement was founded by Guru Nānak in the late 15th century which later came to be known as Sikhism. It emphasised devotion to one God, social equality, and the rejection of rituals. Under Guru Nānak and his successors, the Sikh community expanded and flourished across the subcontinent, particularly in the Punjab, Sindh, and Gangetic plains. During this period, Punjab was largely under the Lodī Sultans and later under the Mughals. It was a fertile, densely populated region, with a large peasant population.

Fig. 3.15. A 19th century image of Sri Harmandir Sahib (Golden Temple), Amritsar

Due to the growing socio-cultural influence of the Sikh Gurus, the Mughal state increasingly intervened in their affairs from the beginning of the 17th century. As the Guru’s influence was increasing, Jahāngīr ordered the execution of Guru Arjan Dev (the fifth Sikh guru) on the pretext of the Guru’s alleged support to Jahāngīr’s rebellious son, Khusrau Mirza. The martyrdom of Guru Arjan Dev marked a significant turning point in the history of the Sikhs and contributed to a gradual shift in their organisational and political orientation. Later, Guru Hargobind (the sixth Sikh Guru) was detained by Jahāngīr in Gwalior Fort. During this period, the Sikhs visited Gwalior in large numbers and paid their respects to the Guru from outside the fortress. In 1675, Aurangzeb ordered the execution of Guru Tegh Bahādur, the ninth Sikh Guru. The martyrdom proved a turning point in Indian history. The brutality of the Mughal state against the Sikh Gurus particularly Guru Arjan Dev, Guru Tegh Bahādur, and the tenth Guru, Guru Gobind Singh has few comparisons in history. The valour and sacrifice of the Gurus for the cause of the faith became the defining features of Sikhism. Beyond political motivations, the act was also deeply rooted in the rigid religious and discriminatory practices of the Mughals. The martyrdom of the Sikh Gurus to uphold their faith and principles firmed the conviction of the Sikhs that persecution had to be met with firm resistance and resilience. Such persecution and political pressure could only be countered through collective organisation, and armed resistance as and when required. Many armed conflicts took place between the Sikhs and the Mughals. Guru Hargobind established the Akāl Takht, a seat of temporal authority, to administer justice and address matters of governance. Scholars suggest that the establishment of the Akāl Takht was intended, in part, to counter Mughal political dominance.

Jat Resistance

During the 17th century, the Jats, a farming community from Ganga– Yamuna Doab were burdened with heavy taxes and exploitation by Mughals. These economic hardships combined with Aurangzeb’s religious policies led to growing resentment amongst the Jats. In 1669, under the leadership of Gokul Jat, they rebelled against the Mughal Empire. Mughal forces struggled for many months to suppress the uprising.

However, the Jats continued their resistance. In 1686, they took advantage of Aurangzeb’s prolonged military campaigns in the Deccan and rebelled once again under the leadership of the zamindars of Sinsani and Sogar. The Mughals sent several armies to suppress the rebellion, leading to a long and fierce struggle in which both sides suffered heavy losses. By 1691, the Mughals had gained the upper hand. Yet, the Jat resistance continued.

During the later part of Akbar’s reign, the philosophy of Sulh-i-kul was adopted as a state policy. It represented ‘peace with all’, ‘universal toleration’, or ‘absolute peace’. Sufism began to expand in the subcontinent with the arrival of the Turks. It was a mystical dimension of Islam that generally adopted a comparatively liberal outlook towards the world. It emerged in West Asia as a form of spiritual response to the worldly orientation of some Ulema (Islamic theologians and jurists) and the orthodoxy of Shariat (Muslim jurisprudence and law). Sufism spread through various orders, or silsilahs. During the Delhi Sultanate, the Chishti and Suhrawardi orders became prominent, while under the Mughals, the Qaddiriyya and Naqshbandiya orders gained importance. Sufi khanqahs (hospices) and shrines developed as centres of popular religious life and also played a role in social and political affairs. Sufis also contributed to the spread of Islam. In India, Sufis adopted many local practices to connect with the wider population, including using Indian languages such as Hindavi and Bengali. Some Sufis also incorporated yogic practices, such as prāṇāyāma, known in Persian as habs-i-dam.

Temples: The Resilience of Sacred Sites

Temples functioned as central pillars of the region’s socio-economic and political life. These religious institutions evolved into major economic hubs that managed extensive land grants, provided employment to hundreds of specialised workers, and served as financial institutions for local traders. Rulers used temple patronage to strengthen their political legitimacy. Beyond their spiritual and religious functions, temples facilitated social integration and served as centres of education and culture, where artistic traditions such as Bharatanatyam and many other art forms were preserved. They also exercised quasi-judicial authority, collecting local taxes and administering legal penalties.

Temples also served as sources of legitimacy for political authority. The deities of these temples were regarded as protectors of the state and its people. In some cases, the deity was regarded as the ruler, while the actual ruler governed as the deity’s deputy. For example, the Gajapati rulers of Odisha called themselves the Rauta (deputy) of Jagannath. Similarly, the Kachhwāhā rulers of Jaipur governed on behalf of Govindadevajī, while the Rajput rulers of Kota regarded themselves as representatives of Rāma.

Temples in India also functioned as significant economic institutions. They provided employment to a wide range of groups, including architects, artisans, sculptors, priests, musicians, and administrative staff. As substantial landholders, temples received donations, endowments, and land grants, often given as acts of

Indian Economy: Agriculture, Trading Networks, and Merchant Communities

The expansion of the agrarian economy and the gradual proliferation of centres of artisanal and craft production were significant developments of the early second millennium CE.

The main source of state income was land revenue, and the expanding frontiers of empires resulted in an increase in the area under cultivation. Over time, the collection of land revenue became increasingly strict and exploitative at various levels of administration. For example, during the reigns of Alauddin Khiljī and Mohammad bin Tughlaq, the land tax was raised to as much as 50 per cent of total agricultural production, in addition to other taxes. Moreover, the Sultans of Delhi, and later the Mughals had limited role in maritime trade. Their concerns regarding coastal areas and port cities were largely confined to political and military matters and, to some extent, the collection of customs duties. As a result, coastal areas and their commerce remained largely in the hands of regional power holders and local communities.

Irrigation technologies such as rope pulleys, spinning wheel, and Persian wheel were widely used by local populations, resulting in enhanced agricultural and economic output. In the Vijayanagara Empire, agricultural production varied greatly in both form and scale. Consequently, irrigation systems ranged from extensive canal networks to small rain-fed patches of land. A defining feature of the Vijayanagara economy was its investment in small-scale irrigation.

The World of the Indian Ocean: Continuity and Change

With the turn of the 16th century, two major developments occurred—the arrival of Europeans for trading ventures and the Mughal conquest of northern India. The Europeans, particularly the Portuguese, and the Mughals entered an already established economic system in which local institutions and networks played a crucial role. The Portuguese attempted to establish greater control over oceanic trade and commerce, while the Mughals sought to centralise control over the land revenue system. European involvement, which began with the Portuguese and was later followed by the Dutch, English, Danes, and French, developed into a complex system of interactions between European trading companies and Indian merchant networks. Subsequently, the Indian subcontinent became a major global intersection of commerce and cross-cultural exchange.

Simultaneously, new merchant groups emerged in coastal and hinterland areas. They controlled wealth, production system, supply chains, and at the same time, funded states and supported their political allies. The peasantry was involved in the production of raw materials, while weavers, dyers, bleachers, and other groups were working regularly to ready the deliveries of cotton textiles. Before the 15th century, the Indian Ocean trading network had a huge presence of Arab, Chinese, and Indian merchants. In subsequent centuries, Gujarati merchants in particular emerged as significant

beneficiaries of the changing commercial order. Alongside them, numerous merchant groups flourished in Malabar, Coromandel, and Bengal, adapting to the growing influence and competition of the British, Dutch, and French.

These merchant groups were not merely involved in the buying and selling of goods, but were closely connected to the political economy, facilitating and financing trade. Indian merchants functioned as traders, shippers, bankers, money-changers, brokers, dubhaśhīs (interpreters and translators), diplomats, and bureaucrats.

Between 1000 and 1700 CE, despite frequent invasions, shifts in political authority, and unrest, the Indian economy remained dynamic. The economy was driven by adaptations of agricultural innovations and irrigation systems, markets, powerful merchant groups, advanced financial instruments like the hunḍī, and India’s pivotal position in transcontinental Indian Ocean trade. This economic strength contributed to resilience and stability, giving people the resources they needed to resist politically and culturally. Thus, this period is best understood as a complex interplay of conflict and cooperation, defined by the deep interconnection between resistance and resilience. The Rajputs, Ahoms, Vijayanagara rulers, Marathas, Sikhs, and Jats, including many others, consistently defended their lands and identities. To safeguard their sovereignty, they used a strategic mix of direct military resistance, highly adaptive guerrilla warfare, knowledge of local terrains, diplomacy, and alliances.
