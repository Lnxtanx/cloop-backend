Life on Earth

The Big Questions

1. How do physiography and climate shape life on Earth?

2. How are humans and other life forms interconnected?

3. What is the importance of preserving various life forms, and how can we contribute to their conservation?

Geography helps us understand how climate, landforms, and water shape life on Earth—and how humans adapt their lives to these conditions. From forests and grasslands to deserts and coastal regions, different environments support different forms of life. At the same time, human activities influence these natural systems in significant ways. This chapter explores the close relationships among physical features, climate, and living organisms, and encourages us to reflect on our responsibility towards preserving the diversity of life on Earth.

Fig. 2.1. Earth as a giant patchwork quilt

Imagine our planet as a giant patchwork quilt, where each patch has its own colours, weather, and living beings. These huge patches are called biomes. The word ‘biome’ is made up of the word bio, derived from the Greek word bios, meaning ‘life’, and -ome, a suffix used in biology to denote a group of a specific type of biological constituents. Biomes are large natural regions of the world where certain types of plants and animals live under similar climatic conditions. For example, icy tundras, hot deserts, green forests, and vast grasslands are all different types of biomes. In simple terms, a biome is like a broad environmental region, while an ecosystem is like a neighbourhood within it, where plants, animals, and non-living things interact closely. Each biome is unique, but together they form the living world of our planet, supporting diverse forms of life and helping maintain the balance of nature.

Terrestrial Biomes

Terrestrial biomes are classified into forests, grasslands, deserts, and tundra, based on their climate.

Forest Biome

A forest biome is like nature’s giant green umbrella—warm, wet, and bursting with life at every level, from the tallest trees to the damp forest floor. Tropical rainforests are mainly found near the Equator, between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn (23.5° N to 23.5° S). These regions remain hot and wet throughout the year, which supports the growth of dense forests. The largest tropical rainforests are located in the Amazon Basin in South America, the Congo Basin in Africa, and parts of Southeast Asia, such as Indonesia and Malaysia. Smaller rainforest regions are also found in Central America, as well as in India’s Andaman & Nicobar Islands, and the Northeastern states. Together, these areas form the world’s richest

Desert Biome

Deserts are the driest biomes, receiving very little rainfall. They can be hot, like the Sahara in Africa, or cold, like the Gobi in Asia. The Rann of Kutch in Gujarat is one of the world’s largest salt deserts, turning into a marshy wetland during the monsoon and a vast white salt desert during the dry season. The extreme conditions of a desert make it difficult for life to survive; however, plants like cacti and animals such as camels, lizards, and snakes are specially adapted to these conditions. They can store water and withstand heat and dryness. Deserts often have sandy soil and sparse vegetation. Despite their harshness, they are home to unique ecosystems, and many of them also provide valuable resources such as minerals and oil.

Tundra Biome

Understanding Society:

The tundra biome is the coldest of all and is found near the Arctic and Antarctic regions. It has very long, harsh winters and short summers. The soil is frozen almost year-round—a condition called permafrost—which makes it difficult for trees to grow. The vegetation is limited to mosses, lichens, and small shrubs. And animals, such as reindeer, arctic foxes, and polar bears, which are well adapted to survive freezing temperatures, are often found here. The tundra is fragile, and even small temperature changes can affect its ecosystem.

Aquatic Biomes

Aquatic biomes cover about three-fourths of the Earth’s surface and are divided into freshwater and marine biomes (Fig.2.9). Freshwater biomes include rivers, lakes, ponds, and wetlands. These are crucial for agriculture, getting access to drinking water, and supporting species, such as fish, frogs, and insects. Marine biomes include oceans, seas, and coral reefs, which are rich in biodiversity and influence the Earth’s climate by storing and distributing heat. They are also home to countless species, including whales, dolphins, fish, and plankton, and provide food and livelihood for millions of people.

Interconnectedness between Biomes and Living Beings

Biomes are closely connected to living beings because they provide a natural environment where plants, animals, and humans can survive and interact. At the same time, living beings also influence biomes—animals help in pollination and seed dispersal, humans modify landscapes for farming or cities, and microorganisms recycle nutrients. In this way, biomes and living beings are interconnected systems that depend on one another to maintain ecological balance and biodiversity. We will discuss some of the aspects of this interconnectedness in the following section.

Maintaining Ecological Balance and Biodiversity

Biodiversity

Biodiversity is the variety of life on Earth, including plants, animals, microorganisms, and the ecosystems they inhabit (Fig. 2.11). It refers not only to the number of different species but also to the genetic differences within species and the variety of habitats and ecological processes that sustain life. In simple terms, biodiversity means the richness of life forms and the natural balance they create, which is essential for maintaining healthy ecosystems and supporting human survival. Fig. 2.11. Biodiversity in wild animals

The geographical aspects of biodiversity conservation focus on how location, climate, landforms, and ecosystems influence the protection and management of living organisms. Since biodiversity is unevenly distributed across the Earth, geography plays a vital role in conservation. Some key aspects of conservation are—

Hotspots of Biodiversity: Certain regions, such as the Himalaya, the Western Ghats, the North–East India, and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands, are rich in unique species found nowhere else. Identifying and protecting these hotspots is a priority in conservation.

Fig. 2.12. (Left to right, top to bottom) Endangered Nilgiri Tahr Wild Goat; Himalayan Snowcock Bird; Indian Spider, Western Ghats; Big Himalayan Yak;

Mangrove Forest, Andaman Islands

Ecosystem-Based Conservation: Different biomes (forests, wetlands, deserts, coasts, and mountains) support distinct life forms. Conservation strategies must suit the geographical setting; for example, mangrove protection along coasts or afforestation in mountains.

Protected Areas: National parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, and World Heritage Sites are chosen based on geography to preserve critical habitats and prevent habitat fragmentation.

Geographical Barriers and Corridors: Natural features like rivers, mountains, and valleys serve as barriers or corridors that influence animal movement and gene flow. Conservation planning uses this knowledge to create wildlife corridors.

Human–Environment Interaction: Geography also considers how local communities use land, water, and forests. Sustainable practices vary with geography, such as terrace farming in the hills or agroforestry in the plains.

Ecotourism in forests refers to responsible travel to forested areas where tourists enjoy nature while contributing to its conservation. Forests are home to a wide variety of plants, animals, rivers, and waterfalls, which attract people who want to experience natural beauty, wildlife, etc. Unlike mass tourism, ecotourism emphasises minimal environmental impact, respect for local communities, and learning about ecosystems. For example, visiting national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, or trekking through rainforests allows visitors to observe biodiversity while supporting conservation programmes and generating income for the local people. Thus, ecotourism in forests helps balance tourism, environmental protection, and sustainable development.

Forests and Livelihoods

Fig. 2.15. Rituals to honour the forest deities

People, particularly rural and tribal communities, rely heavily on forests for their livelihoods. Forests provide essential resources such as food, fuel, fodder, medicine, shelter, timber, bamboo, and firewood. Non-timber forest products, such as honey, fruits, nuts, resins, and medicinal plants, help families meet daily needs and earn income. Several communities including the Gonds and Bhils perform rituals to honour the forest deities before collecting resources, reflecting sustainable cultural practices and a deep respect for nature (Fig. 2.15). Forests also support livestock grazing, small forest-based industries, ecotourism, and employment, making them vital for the livelihood, culture, and survival of many.

Geographical Indication (GI)

Did you know that some forest products are unique to specific regions and are recognised as Geographical Indications (GI)? A Geographical Indication (GI) tag is a type of intellectual property right that identifies a product as originating from a specific place, where a given quality, reputation, or other characteristics of the product are essentially linked to its geographic origin.

In simple terms, it is a label that indicates that a product comes from a particular region and derives its uniqueness from that place. Thus, GI tagging helps protect region-specific products, promote cultural heritage, and generate economic benefits for local communities through exclusive market recognition.

Challenges Faced by People Living in Forests

People living in forests often struggle to access essential facilities such as healthcare, education, and clean water. Women are exposed to fuelwood fumes and face, many health issues, making their daily lives difficult. Forest dwellers depend heavily on forest produce such as firewood, honey, bamboo, and medicinal plants for their livelihood. Restrictions on forest use, deforestation, and the commercial exploitation of resources often limit their access to these resources. This creates a conflict between conservation laws and their survival needs, leading to economic insecurity.

Forested hilly regions are prone to natural disasters such as floods and landslides. Floods can wash away homes, crops, and roads, while landslides can bury villages and cut off access to essential services. Forest fires can quickly spread through dense vegetation, destroying homes and resources. Human care and preventive measures can help prevent many forest fires.

Crops are often damaged by elephants, monkeys, or wild boars, and sometimes people face direct attacks. Such conflicts not only cause economic losses but also create fear and insecurity among forest communities.

Many forest dwellers also face discrimination and neglect from mainstream society. They are often engaged in selling forest products at relatively low prices. Therefore, creating opportunities for processing and marketing these products is essential to enhance their income. Forest communities also require assistance in accessing government schemes and programmes, as well as greater awareness of their legal rights.

Government Efforts to Support Forest Dwellers

To reduce the hardships of people living in forests, the Government of India has taken several steps. One of the most important measures is the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006, commonly called the Forest Rights Act (FRA). The Act recognises the rights of eligible communities over the forest land and forest resources that they have traditionally depended upon. It allows them to collect minor forest produce such as bamboo, honey, and medicinal plants, and grants ownership of small patches of land cultivated by them.

Along with the FRA, the Government runs schemes for tribal welfare, including education scholarships, healthcare programmes, and skill development initiatives. The Joint Forest Management (JFM) programme encourages local communities to participate in protecting forests while benefiting from forest produce. Other initiatives, such as the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act (PESA), the Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan (DAJGUA), and the Pradhan Mantri Janjati Adivasi Nyaya Maha Abhiyan (PM JANMAN), aim to promote the welfare of such communities.

Mendha Lekha: A Case Study in Community Forest Management

Mendha Lekha is a small tribal village located in the Gadchiroli district of Maharashtra, known for its effective community- based forest management and self-governance. The village, inhabited mainly by the Gond tribe, became the first in India to gain rights over its forest resources under the Forest Rights Act (2006). The people of Mendha Lekha follow the principle of Mawa Nate Mawa Raj (our village, our rule), ensuring that all decisions are made collectively through the Gram Sabha. They protect and sustainably utilise their 1,800 hectares of forest by regulating tree cutting, preventing forest fires, and promoting bamboo harvesting as a renewable resource. The revenue from the forest produce is used for community welfare activities such as education and infrastructure. This also upholds the fundamental rights of the people to participate in governance and control resources that sustain their livelihoods. Simultaneously, the villagers fulfil their fundamental duties by protecting biodiversity and preventing illegal exploitation. This model has not only strengthened local livelihoods but also demonstrates how traditional knowledge, rights, and duties, as well as democratic participation, can coexist to achieve sustainable forest conservation (Fig. 2.22).

Ecological Commons—Shared Resources

The term ecological commons refer to natural resources like forests, rivers, lakes, grazing lands, air, and biodiversity that are shared by a community and used collectively for its survival and well-being. Unlike private property, these resources are not owned by any single individual but belong to the community as a whole, and are often managed through traditional rules and practices. For centuries, people have depended on the ecological commons for food, water, fuel, fodder, and medicinal plants. However, with population growth, industrial use, and privatisation, many of these commons are shrinking or degrading. Protecting ecological commons is important not only for the livelihoods of rural and tribal communities but also for maintaining ecological balance, regulating climate, and conserving biodiversity.

A Case Study of Commons-based Fisheries Management in Bihar

After independence, The Indian Fisheries Act, 1897, remained the basis for the governance of inland fisheries. Historically, control over perennial water bodies and diara waterfronts in Bihar was held by large landowners who marginalised the fishing community, rendering them the most vulnerable group in an environment abundant with aquatic resources.

Case Study of Water Tanks Built by the Gond Kings

In India, for centuries, regions with seasonal rainfall have tackled the issue of water demand and management as a collective concern and used common public resources for the same. In fact, between 800 and 1200 CE, the Chandella and Bundela kings built around 8,000 tanks in the Bundelkhand region. Similarly, keres were built in Karnataka, eri tanks in Tamil Nadu, and chevuru tanks in Andhra Pradesh and Odisha. Bihar had the famous ahar pynes to store water and the Gond kings funded huge katas—three-sided reservoirs— in the Gondwana region. According to official estimates, there are more than 15 lakh functional tanks in India at present.
