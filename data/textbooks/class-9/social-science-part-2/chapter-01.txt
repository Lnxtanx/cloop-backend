Ocean Relief

As we explore human–ocean connections, let us begin with the ocean floor and its features. Just as land has mountains, valleys, and plains, the ocean floor, too, has several landforms, together known as submarine relief or ocean relief. These include underwater mountains, valleys, plateaus, and trenches. The Mariana Trench in the Pacific Ocean is the deepest place on Earth, while the Mid-Ocean Ridge is the world’s longest mountain range, hidden beneath the ocean. Some underwater mountains like Mauna Kea (Hawai‘i) are even taller than Mount Everest.

During storms, strong winds can generate huge waves that may cause significant damage along coastlines. Tides

Hydraulic action: The erosion of rock and soil caused by the force of moving water, which loosens and removes material.

Tides are the rhythmic rise and fall of ocean water that occur twice a day in most parts of the world. When the water rises to its highest level and covers much of the shore, it is called high tide (flood tide). When the water falls to its lowest level and moves away from the shore, it is called low tide (ebb tide). Observe Fig. 1.5 and note the change in water level during high and low tides. You may wonder what causes this regular rise and fall of ocean water. To understand this, let us explore how tides are formed.

Spring tides: Observe Fig. 1.5(a), where the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon are aligned in a straight line. Spring tides occur during this alignment, which happens on full moon and new moon days. At this time, the combined gravitational pull of the Sun and the Moon causes high tides of unusual heights and low tides that are unusually lower. Sun and the Moon acts in different directions. As a result, the difference between high tide and low tide becomes smaller, producing neap tides. Fig. 1.6 shows the levels of high and low water during both spring and neap tides, along with the average low-water levels for each.

High tides are important for navigation because they raise the water level near the shore, allowing ships to enter harbours more easily. In ports and coastal areas, tidal information is essential, as tidal ranges in some ports can reach 4–5 metres. The Gulf of Khambhat has one of the highest tidal ranges, at about 12 metres. Before a ship berths at a port, it must check both the water depth and the tidal range to ensure safe docking, especially during low tide.

Some sea channels, such as the Singapore Strait, contain shallow areas. Hence, ships such as Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs) passing through these regions often wait for hours to transit only during high tide to ensure sufficient water depth and avoid running aground.

Tidal changes also generate strong currents because large volumes of water flow in and out of coastal areas. These tidal currents can reach speeds of 5–6 knots (about 9–11 km/h).

Ocean currents are continuous streams of water that flow across the ocean surface in definite directions (Fig. 1.9). They are caused by factors, such as winds, the Earth’s rotation (Coriolis force), differences in temperature and salinity, and the shape of continents and ocean basins. Ocean currents may be warm or cold depending on the direction from which the water flows. Currents flowing from warmer regions are called warm currents, while those flowing from colder regions are called cold currents. On maps, warm currents are usually shown in red and cold currents in blue.

The Gulf Stream, a warm current originating in the Gulf of Mexico in the Atlantic Ocean, flows towards Norway and keeps its waters relatively warm. As a result, sea ice does not form easily in the Norwegian region despite its high latitude (60°–70°). In contrast, the Labrador Current, a cold current flowing along the east coast of Canada, carries freezing water and supports the formation of sea ice and icebergs even at lower latitudes.

Cyclones and Tsunamis—Early Warning Systems

Oceans and seas can give rise to sudden natural hazards such as cyclones, which are large rotating storms, and tsunamis, which are giant waves caused by earthquakes. To protect people from such dangers, scientists have developed Early Warning Systems that work like giant alarm systems for coastal areas. To reduce risks, people, communities, and governments must act in advance. Early Warning Systems help detect cyclones and tsunamis, predict disasters, and issue timely warnings so that people can evacuate and stay safe.

India has several Cyclone Warning Services and Marine Weather Services that monitor storms along both the eastern and western coasts. For early warning systems to be effective, they must function continuously. India uses advanced technologies to predict cyclones at an early stage, which has significantly reduced the loss of human and animal lives.

The Government of India has launched the National Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project (NCRMP) to build cyclone shelters, strengthen warning systems, and improve emergency response mechanisms. The project focuses on cyclone-prone States and Union Territories and includes a Decision Support System (DSS) for disaster management authorities. The Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS), in collaboration with the India Meteorological Department (IMD), has also established the Storm Surge Early Warning System (SSEWS) for the Indian coasts. Its main objective is to protect coastal communities by forecasting cyclone-induced storm surges and the extent of coastal inundation.

Marine Resources and Their Significance

Do you recall learning about natural resources in the Grade 8 Social Science Part 1 textbook? These resources are obtained from land, water, forests and animals, and other natural sources. Oceans, too, are vast storehouses of resources, and the materials and features found in oceans that have intrinsic or economic value are called marine resources. Marine resources can be broadly classified into two categories—Marine Living Resources and Marine Non-living Resources. They include fish and seafood, biodiversity, oil and natural gas, minerals, sand and gravel, renewable blue energy sources such as tidal and wave energy, tourism potential, and unique ecosystems such as coral reefs. Innocent Passage is a principle that allows foreign ships to pass through the territorial waters of a country as long as they do not threaten its peace, good order, or security. Ships must move continuously without stopping, except in emergencies or cases of breakdowns. This right does not permit activities such as fishing, military operations, or other non-peaceful actions.

Beyond the contiguous zone, lie the Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs), which extend from the baseline up to 200 nautical miles from the coast, and are defined under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) by global agreement. In these zones, a country has special rights to use ocean resources, such as fish, oil, and gas, although ships from other countries may still pass through peacefully. It is important to note, however, that the country does not fully own or control the waters of the EEZ.

Beyond the EEZs of all countries lie the high seas, also known as international waters. Covering about 64 per cent of the world’s oceans, these waters do not belong to any single country and are therefore considered part of the global commons. All countries enjoy equal rights on the high seas, including navigation, fishing, flying over them, laying undersea cables, building lawful structures such as artificial lands, and exploring for minerals.

Although the high seas are essential, they also present many challenges. Since no single country controls them, issues, such as overfishing, illegal fishing, and conflicts over resources often arise. These problems affect millions of people who depend on the sea for food and livelihoods. Ensuring navigation and safety is also more difficult without clear enforcement mechanisms. On the high seas, the laws of the flag state—the country in which a ship is registered—apply.

International Maritime Rules and Regulations

In the earlier sections of this chapter, you learnt that oceans are vital for trade, travel, and communication. To ensure that activities at sea remain safe, fair, and peaceful, countries follow a set of international rules and agreements known as maritime law or maritime regulations. These laws regulate how ships operate and move; protect the marine environment; address issues such as piracy, terrorism, natural disasters, and accidents; safeguard trade routes; and help settle disputes between countries.

Maritime disputes are conflicts or disagreements between countries over territory, resources, or rights in naval areas such as oceans, seas, or coastal regions. Can you find out some countries that have conflicts over their territorial sea waters?

Important Maritime Rules and Regulations

Just as there are traffic rules on land, ships must follow “rules of the road at sea” to avoid accidents. These rules, known as COLREGs (Convention on the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea), include maintaining a proper lookout, deciding which side to pass on, identifying the give-way and stand-on vessels, proceeding at a safe speed, and using lights, sound signals, shapes (for different types of vessels such as fishing boats), along with appropriate actions to prevent collisions. In addition, ships use international signals and radio communication to share information and prevent accidents, especially in crowded or hazardous waters.

Ships must also meet safety standards and are prohibited from polluting the seas. The MARPOL (International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships) Convention is an important international agreement aimed at reducing pollution from ships.

Why are Maritime Rules Important?

International maritime rules, regulations, and agreements help keep the oceans safe and ensure the fair and sustainable use of ocean resources. These rules enable countries to cooperate in using marine resources without conflict while protecting marine life, ships, sailors, and coastal communities. Without such regulations, the oceans could become dangerous and chaotic, with increased pollution, accidents, collisions, and disputes over resources. By establishing common standards for all ships, regardless of the country they belong to, maritime rules help keep the oceans safe, clean, and open for trade and travel.
