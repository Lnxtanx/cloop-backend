The choice of materials, tools, and techniques has shaped every artist’s expression throughout history. Think of the range of surfaces and materials from the prehistoric cave paintings made with earth pigments to the digital drawing devices of today! Many artworks have survived changing weather and climatic conditions as well as other external threats and damage. The fact that we can still see and experience artworks that were made thousands of years ago proves that artists had deep knowledge and advanced techniques of using various materials in art processes.

Care, Maintenance, and Responsible Storage

of Materials

It is important to be organised and careful while using and storing materials. This not only ensures that they can be shared with others but also preserves the vibrancy and longevity of artworks. Here are some useful practices that can be followed:

Source consciously

Choose non-toxic and sustainable materials as much as possible.

Reuse paper and experiment with recyclable materials.

Handle and maintain with care

Clean brushes immediately after use. Wash them with soap and rinse thoroughly. Shape their bristles and dry them horizontally or upright (with bristles facing up).

Close paint tubes tightly and properly, and replace the cap of markers and ink bottles after using to prevent drying.

Do not touch artworks with dirty or oily hands.

Dispose of inks, acrylics, and chemical waste like turpentine responsibly.

Fig. 15.2. Care and maintenance

Responsible storage

Visit a stationery store or an art supplies store to see how paper, books, and other stationery are stored carefully in a limited space.

Store paper, canvas, and drawings in dry, flat spaces.

Store sharp objects like cutters, scissors, needles, and sculpting tools safely in a box at a safe space, away from young children, animals, and vulnerable others.

Fig. 15.6. Śholāpith, West Bengal

Śholāpith is a crafts tradition practised in West Bengal and Odisha. A similar tradition practised in Tamil Nadu is called Neṭṭī. Artists of this tradition extract a milky-white spongy core (pith) of the śholā plant (scientific name: Aeschynomene aspera) to make intricately carved decorative objects. Traditionally, śholāpith was used to craft garlands, headgear for brides and grooms, as well as idols of deities, and as decorations during festivities. The craft is practised by artisans called malakars (garland-makers), although they craft much more than garlands these days.

Explore Mediums with Colour Theory

The Chitra-sūtra of the Viṣhṇu-dharmottara Purāṇa elaborates on every aspect of sourcing, extracting, preparing, and using colours. It lists the basic colours as white, yellow, red, blue, and black. When these are mixed, they produce innumerable colours.

The text also mentions that the secondary colours yield many variations of tints and shades by adding varying amounts of white and black respectively.

Perspective Techniques

Perspective is a drawing technique that is used to show the three-dimensionality or spatial depth on a two-dimensional surface. There are two kinds of perspective techniques, which are explained below.

Linear perspective

This is the method where lines that are parallel in reality are drawn to converge towards a vanishing point on the horizon. For example, the parallel lines of a corridor are drawn to converge in the distance, which creates the illusion of spatial depth (Fig. 15.8).

Fig. 15.8. Linear perspective

Aerial perspective

Also known as atmospheric perspective, aerial perspective uses colour tonalities to depict distance and depth.

In this landscape painting, the mountain on the right side of the painting is painted with richer colours than the mountains in the distance, which appear to be hazy behind a layer of mist. The plants and trees that are closer to the viewer are painted with more detail than those in the distance.
