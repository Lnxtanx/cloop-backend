Dance as Visual Storytelling 11

Many Indian dance forms are deeply rooted in the tradition of storytelling. Often described as dṛiśhya-kāvya, or visual poetry, Indian dance uses movement, gesture, and facial expression to communicate meaning clearly and powerfully. Through this expressive tradition, dancers bring to life a wide range of narratives — from classical literary works such as Kālidāsa’s Abhijñāna-Śhākuntalam to modern interpretations of Shakespeare’s Romeo and Juliet; the rhythms of seasonal cycles and the journey from sowing seeds to harvest, scenes from everyday life, and the lived experiences of people.

Dance as Visual Storytelling

Āṅgika Abhinaya

It refers to using the body as a tool for communication and articulation in dance. Every dance form has its own movement vocabulary and signature movements that distinguish it from others. The āṅgika can include various types of stylised movements such as sthāna, chārī, gati, utplavana, bhramarī, and hastas, which you can recall from previous grades.

Vāchika Abhinaya

It refers to the use of verbal elements such as speech, poetry, lyrics, narration, and dialogue for expressive communication in dance. This even includes the recitation of rhythmic syllables. Through Vāchika Abhinaya, the dancer communicates the essence of the story or shares the energy of the performance. These aural elements create the soundscape that shapes a dance’s movement vocabulary.

Experience dance through sound, song, and silence

Dance with instrumental music: Perform signature movements of any regional dance that uses only instrumental music. You may also use claps or rhythmic syllables such as dha, ta, ka, dhin, or create your own syllables while dancing. Here are a few examples for you — Tārpā dance (Maharashtra), Diṁsā dance (Andhra Pradesh), Kaikoṭṭikaḻi (Kerala), and Rāut Nāchā (Chhattisgarh). Dance with lyrical music: Perform the signature movements of a regional dance form that uses songs with lyrics. For example, Bihu (Assam), Ghoomar (Rajasthan), Dhamāl (Haryana), and Rāuf (Kashmir). Dance to your own singing: Sing a regional song learnt in your music class and perform simple movements in any dance style of your choice.

Āhārya Abhinaya

Fig. 11.8. Students performing in

different costumes

It is the use of āhārya (visual elements) such as costumes, make-up, and props to convey visual narratives, cultural context, and mood in dance. A striking feature of any dance is its costume and make-up. Often, a dance form can be identified simply by its costume, as it reflects the region’s textile and jewellery traditions. Props may also support rhythmic patterns. For example, the Lezim with its metal cymbals provides aural accompaniment to the energetic Lezim dance of Maharashtra, while the chikkā, a wooden clapper used in Bhangra from Punjab, adds both visual and rhythmic complexity. In harvest dances, movements often imitate farming activities and representative props may be used.
