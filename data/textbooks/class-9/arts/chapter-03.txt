Symbolism and Metaphors in Theatre

Symbolism in theatre is when an object, action, colour, sound, or movement represents something deeper than its literal meaning. For example:

A single lamp on stage might symbolise hope.

A closed door might symbolise fear or opportunity.

A red light could symbolise passion or energy.

Fig. 3.2. Symbolism on stage

Metaphors in theatre are devices used by playwrights to draw comparisons between two seemingly dissimilar things or ideas.

For example:

A character walking in circles might be a metaphor for confusion or being trapped.

A torn costume might be a metaphor for a broken identity.

Metaphors help the story speak in images, not just words.

Why are they necessary

Symbolism and metaphors help theatre move beyond simple dialogue to become emotionally powerful and visually expressive. They allow:

1. Deeper meaning: Instead of directly stating ideas, symbols let the audience discover meaning on their own. It makes the experience richer and more memorable.

2. Emotional connection: It can express what words cannot — fear, love, loss. This connects with the audience’s imagination.

52

3. Creativity on stage: Theatre often has limited sets or props. Symbolism allows a single object or movement to represent many ideas, making storytelling more flexible and artistic, like the lamp or the closed door mentioned above.

4. Cultural expression: In Indian theatre, especially in traditions like Kathakali and Yakṣhagāna, gestures, colours, and costumes are symbolic.

5. Visual impact: Symbolic images stay with viewers long after the play ends — like a candle blowing out at the final moment, or a character handing over a flower to show forgiveness.

So, symbolism and metaphors turn theatre into a language of images, feelings, and ideas, making performances meaningful and unforgettable.

Symbolism Using Music

Music is a very powerful tool that can transform how one experiences and interprets a scene.

For example, when a character enters the stage, if the sound of a snake hissing plays in the background, the audience will associate that character to be dangerous … like a snake.

However, if there is a soothing flute playing in the background when they enter, the character will be accepted as calm and endearing.

Musical symbolism doesn’t have to be restricted to just the person. The instruments used in the theme music determine the mood and the underlying meaning of the play.

Though some of these arise from the culture and practices associated with the region, there are a lot of sounds that are more universal, based on the human psychology. The Nāṭyaśhāstra suggests the swaras that evoke certain rasas in this stanza:

हास््यशृङ्गारययोः कार्यौ स््वरौ मध््यमपञ््चमौ । षड्जर््षभौ चच कर््तव््यौ ववीररौद्ाद्भुतेष््वथ ॥ ९९ ॥

गान््धारश्च निषादश्च कर््तव््यौ करुणे रसे । धधैवतश्चैवव कर््तव््यो बीभत्से समययानके ॥ १०० ॥

Hāsyaśhṛṅgārayoḥ kāryau svarau madhyamapañchamau|

Ṣhaḍjaṛiṣhabhau cha kartavyau vīraraudrādbhuteṣhvatha|| 99 ||

Gāndhāraśhcha niṣhādaśhcha kartavyau karuṇe rase|

Dhaivataśhchaiva kartavyo bībhatse samayānake|| 100 ||

Nāṭyaśhāstra, Ch. 17 (Kāvyamālā edn.)

A song in the love and the comic sentiments should abound in many madhyamas (M) and pañchamas (P), and in the heroic, the furious and the marvellous sentiments, songs should be made with many ṣhadja (S) and ṛishabas (R). And the song in the pathetic sentiment should be full of many gandhāras (G) and niṣhāda (N). Similarly, a song in the odious and the terrible sentiments should have many dhaivatas (D).

55

To evoke feelings of joy, playfulness, and love, the notes madhyama (M) and pañchama (P) should be emphasised. These notes are believed to convey a sense of lightness, agility, and delight. For expressing courage, anger, or amazement, the notes ṣhaḍja (S) and ṛiṣhabha (R) are primarily used. These notes symbolise strength, stability, and intense energy. ​To convey grief, sorrow, or deep empathy, the notes gāndhāra (G) and niṣhāda (N) should be employed. Their natural resonance carries an inherent softness and a ‘melting’ quality that evokes compassion. To create an atmosphere of dread, fear, or revulsion, the prominence of the note dhaivata (D) is prescribed.

Symbolism Using Costumes

The type of costume can suggest everything about the world in which the story is set, the time period, country, social settings, and so on.

Style and texture: Heavy, rich fabrics may suggest royalty; simple cotton indicates humility or innocence. Flowing costumes can show softness or dream-like qualities while sharp, structured lines suggest rigidity.

Transformation through costume: A change in costume (or even an added layer) often symbolises a character’s internal change or a shift in the story.

Example: A character is going in and out of his house multiple times. The first time the character is wearing loose cotton clothes, the second time a raincoat, and the third time the character is wearing woollen clothes with gloves. This symbolises a change in the weather and passage of time.

Symbolism Using Make-up

Age and experience: While wrinkles or grey shading can show wisdom or burden, smooth, bright make-up may represent youth or innocence.

Cultural or traditional symbols: Certain markings or motifs represent identity, heritage, or spiritual qualities.

Fig. 3.8. Symbolic make-up

Abstract Make-up: Make-up can express themes like chaos, harmony, duality (for example, half of the face painted differently).

Example: A character narrating the journey of her life entering and exiting from time to time. Every time she re-enters the stage, her hair are whiter and ageing lines are deeper. Hair and the lines are symbolic of the passage of time.

Note to the teacher: Please ensure that all make-up sessions are conducted with proper safety and hygiene measures. Students should use skin-safe, non-allergenic products. Care must be taken while using brushes and other equipment near sensitive areas like the eyes.

Symbolism Using Light

Lights not only provide visibility, but are also instrumental in telling the audience what to see, where to see, and what it means. Lights can communicate emotion, theme, mood, and hidden meaning as well. There are various factors that contribute to creating these effects.

Intensity (brightness)

How bright or dim the light is affects the mood.

Bright light: Clarity, revelation, happiness

Dim light: Fear, secrecy, confusion, suspense

Sudden blackouts: Shock, transitions, dramatic endings

Direction and angles

‘Where’ the light comes from changes the symbolic meaning.

Footlight: Fear, supernatural effects

Backlight: Mystery, silhouette, inner struggle

Front light: Honesty, clear emotions, openness

Shadows

It is one of the strongest symbols in theatre.

Long shadows: Fear, loneliness, internal conflict

Soft shadows: Memory, nostalgia

No shadows: Honesty, clarity, safety Fig. 3.11. A play of shadows
