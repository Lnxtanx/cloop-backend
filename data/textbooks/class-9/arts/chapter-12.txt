Cultural Connections Through Dance

Culture connects people beyond boundaries. Our customs, social traditions, regional ideas, and beliefs together shape our culture. People from different cultures can connect and develop mutual respect through shared appreciation of diverse dance traditions. For example, dance forms like Dandiya and Bhangra have travelled beyond our borders with the Indian diaspora and are now being enjoyed across the world. They are no longer limited to a single community and have become part of the wider local culture wherever they are performed. Indian dance forms have also been adopted by international dancers promoting cross-cultural relations.

A Dance for Every Situation

Dance is a reflection of human experiences. From birth to death, you can find dances associated with every aspect of life in India. Many dances are performed to celebrate key moments in life — Badhāī of Madhya Pradesh and Sohar of Bihar are performed to celebrate birth, while Khoriyā of Haryana and Chholiyā Nṛitya of the Kumaon region of Uttarakhand are performed at weddings and in processions. Naṭa- Saṅkīrtana of Manipur is an integral part of funeral rituals in the Vaishnavite communities of the region. Nāṭī of Himachal Pradesh is a colourful dance performed at weddings, among other celebrations. Jhūmar of Bihar is a celebratory dance that is performed year around.

Bārḍo Chham from Arunachal Pradesh symbolises the journey between death and rebirth and is presented as the victory of good over evil. India also has numerous harvest festival dances, such as Bihu from Assam and Hojāgirī from Tripura. Taraṅgmel of Goa is a vibrant community dance in which performers carry colourful flags as props and move to various percussion instruments such as the dhol and tāśhā. Often, the dancers rhythmically, and in unison, cry out the words ‘ho ho’, which energises them and helps maintain coordination. The Ummattāṭ from Karnataka begins with an invocation to the river Kaveri and has its origins in harvest festivals.

Activity 12.1

Journalling your own dance culture

Find information on the folk, tribal, popular, classical, and contemporary dances from:

• your State/ Union Territory of residence; and 
• any State/ Union Territory that does not share a geographic boundary with your State/ Union Territory of residence.

Organise the information and photographs you collect into your journal. Write about the different details of the dance practice, such as the instruments, songs, recurring syllables, and costumes associated with the dance, as well as the occasions for performance, venues, etc.

Activity 12.2

Become a part of your chosen dance culture

Divide yourselves into groups of four or five. Discuss amongst each other and choose two dance forms, one from your region and one from a different region, based on the dances you have chosen. Using videos and other available resources, learn dance movements from the two dance forms that you have researched in Activity 12.1. Discuss the signature movements and other aspects of the dance that are appealing to you within the group.

As a group, introduce the dance forms to the rest of the class and present short performances of the two dance forms.

Activity 12.3

Cross-cultural collaboration in dance

Create and perform a short dance piece, combining elements from two dance forms selected by your group. You may continue with the selection made by your group in Activity 12.2 or choose two other dance forms. Activity 12.4

Dance voices — be a dance podcaster/ interviewer

This activity has three stages: Pre-production, Production, Post- production

Pre-production: Involves conducting background research and planning for the interview

Compile a list of dance schools, institutions, organisations, and well-known dance artists in your region and neighbouring regions. Remember that a dance school or institution is not only a place to learn dance but also a cultural resource centre for the local community. Prepare a set of questions about the functioning of a dance organisation or the life/ career journey of a dance artist or a dance teacher. Encourage curiosity and open-ended inquiry. Every question helps uncover something new and adds to the learning process.

Production: Focuses on conducting and recording the interview

Visit the selected dance school/ institution/ organisation/ dance artist in a group along with a teacher. Assign roles: Some members act as podcast interviewers, while others handle lighting, recording, and technical support. Conduct the interview preferably in video format; if not possible, an audio recording may be used.

Post-production: Involves shaping the recorded material into a polished podcast and highlighting key moments from the experience

If it’s possible, edit the recorded content to create the final dance podcast. You may also create short excerpt videos or highlight key moments and important learnings from the interaction, especially inspirational insights shared by the artist that can be applied to daily life.

One Story, Many Renditions

Do you know?

Trade and travel from the eastern shores of Bharat since the first

century BCE led to significant cultural, religious, and linguistic exchanges. It is believed that the region of Kaliṅga has records of Bāli-yātrā from the third century BCE, which is a festival marking the beginning of trade voyages that commenced after the monsoons, on Kārtika-Pūrṇimā. Even today, Bāli-jātrā is celebrated in Odisha with vibrant songs and dances, keeping alive the memory of those ancient maritime traditions.

From the seventh to the fourteenth century CE, a shared movement and performance vocabulary began to emerge across Southeast Asia.

Activity 12.5

A creative Rāmāyaṇa presentation

In groups of three or four, select any episode from the Rāmāyaṇa and present it through a dance sequence in a style of the group’s choice. Each group member may play a different role or character from the chosen episode. As you choreograph, explore whether elements from different Rāmāyaṇa presentations can be thoughtfully incorporated into your interpretation, such as characteristic gestures, group formations, musical patterns, costumes, or storytelling styles. The groups may choose any movement vocabulary or a combination of styles they are comfortable with to best present the episode.
