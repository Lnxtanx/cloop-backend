Note to the Teacher

The chapters in Theatre are designed to nurture creative thinking. Theatre thrives on exploration, interpretation, and imagination rather than a search for correct or incorrect answers. There is rarely a single ʻrightʼ way to perform a scene or express an idea. Instead, students are encouraged to experiment, question, interpret, and discover their own unique responses. As teachers, your role is not to evaluate creativity through rigid parameters, but to create a safe and encouraging environment where students feel free to try, fail, improvise, and grow.

This book also intends to guide students towards discovering a vision and purpose for theatre. Beyond performance skills, it encourages them to see theatre as a meaningful medium to express ideas, reflect on society, and contribute responsibly. Through creative engagement, students can learn to voice concerns, celebrate strengths, build empathy, and participate thoughtfully in their communities. Thus, theatre becomes not only an art form, but a pathway to becoming aware and responsible citizens.

Teaching theatre does not require expensive infrastructure or elaborate equipment. An empty classroom is more than enough. A cleared space can transform into a forest, a courtroom, a battlefield, or a future city through imagination alone. School corridors can become processional pathways; playgrounds can host ensemble performances; the quadrangle can serve as an open-air stage. When students learn to see performance possibilities in everyday spaces, they develop adaptability and innovation.

At the Grade 9 level, students are capable of understanding depth, symbolism, and layered storytelling. Wherever possible, consider inviting experts — actors, directors, designers — or arranging visits to professional theatre spaces. Such exposure to professional environments will help them appreciate the craft more deeply.

Most importantly, remember that each child brings a different strength to the theatre space. Some may excel in acting, others in writing, designing, organising, or observing. Not every student needs to shine in every aspect. Help them identify their individual skills and encourage meaningful contribution. Theatre is collaborative — and every role matters.

A Journal of My Theatre Journey

Journalling is a powerful habit in theatre. Every rehearsal, performance, mistake, and breakthrough carries lessons that may be forgotten unless they are reflected upon.

Key points to include in your journal

Though your journal is a free space for you to express, here is a list of points that will help you put down your thoughts. Here is a sample:

What Challenged Me What Challenged Me

12 August – Improvisation Exercise: “At the Bus Stop” (Group Scene)

What We Did:

I wanted to make my anxiety very obvious, but the teacher asked me to reduce my movements and try smaller gestures. That was uncomfortable but may be it helped the scene feel more natural.

Worked in groups of five to create a scene where strangers wait at a bus stop. We had to communicate emotions only through body language and expressions.

Feedback I Received Feedback I Received

Good use of body language

Need to slow down movements

I played a nervous student. I was checking my watch repeatedly and tapping my foot to show anxiety.

What I Learned What I Learned

Maintain stronger focus on co- actors instead of the audience

One Memorable Moment One Memorable Moment

One actor pretended to drop their bag, and everyone reacted without planning. That spontaneous moment felt very real and exciting.

I realised that small actions can communicate emotions without words. I understood how important it is to observe other actors. When one of us changed the approach, the whole scene changed!

Dos Don’ts

Don’t copy others’ reflections. Every theatre journey is unique.

Write honestly about your feelings and struggles, not what the teacher wants to hear.

Be consistent: Write regularly. Small entries are better than long last-minute writing.

Focus on personal learning and insights. Avoid generic lines like ‘it was nice’, ‘it was good’.

Reflect deeply: Go beyond ‘what happened’. Write why it mattered to you.

Don’t skip difficult experiences. Challenges are usually the biggest learning moments.

Don’t treat it as a homework task — treat it as a personal artistic record.

Process of making purposeful theatre

Step 1: Define your focus

Each group chooses one social theme based on discussion. For example:

What role do festivals play in building community bonds?

What makes young people feel hopeful today?

Think of topics that are most relevant to you and the people around you. This becomes your project focus.

Step 2: Framing questions

Asking the right question is key! How you frame the question depends on the kind of tool you use and the person who is answering. You must prepare a set of questions, which can be of two types:

1. Open-ended questions allow people to speak freely in their own words. They encourage storytelling, memories, opinions, personal struggles, and experiences that may not be visible on the surface. Example: Can you share a situation where you felt misunderstood?

2. Close-ended questions offer limited, fixed options such as Yes/ No or multiple choices. They help collect clear, measurable information that can be easily counted and compared. Example: Does your community celebrate festivals that include theatre or folk performances? Yes/ No.

Using both types of questions gives you a holistic picture.

Note to the teacher: Encourage each team to frame two open-ended questions and two close-ended questions.

Step 3: Meet people and gather information

When you meet people, you will need to practise active listening and keen observation.

You can use the following methods to learn more about the people around you.

1. Questionnaires and surveys: Useful for collecting perspectives from a larger group

2. Interviews: Conversations that allow emotional depth and honest sharing. This can be chosen for a small group of people, especially dignitaries or experts.

3. Observation: Watching and recording behaviour in everyday life 
— in markets, schools, workplaces.
