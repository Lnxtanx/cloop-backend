13 Dance Choreography

Just as a river’s flow is directed around rocks and pebbles, choreography shapes the way a dance flows. Choreography is the art of creating and arranging dance movements to form a visual pattern or spectacle. The placement of dancers, their directions, pauses, and movements are decisions choreographers make to bring their vision to life.

Do you know?

The first known thinker on the idea of choreography in India is Sage Bhārata, the author of the Nāṭyaśhāstra. More than 2000 years ago, he described a concept called ‘Piṇḍībandhas’, which are group formations characterised by clustering, weaving, linking, and moving in coordinated shapes.

Many of these ancient ideas appear in choreographies even today, such as:

group formations and shapes; coordinated travelling patterns; and movement flow in unison or in opposition.

Choreography and Collaboration

In the context of Indian dance, one would use the term ‘nṛitya- saṁrachanā’ (dance creation) to refer to dance choreography.

A choreography includes the following:

Concept: A clear subject, story, or theme that the dance will explore (for example, seasons, friendships, journeys, celebrations, emotions, or socially relevant ideas)

Style: The chosen genre of presentation (for example, theatrical, lyrical, fantasy- based, narrative, folk-inspired, or abstract)

Movement Vocabulary: The dance style(s) used in the piece (for example, classical, folk, contemporary, freestyle, or a combination)

Time: Decisions about rhythm, tempo, pauses, and timing that influence the mood and pacing of the choreography

Space: Use of formations, directions, spatial levels, distances between dancers, and patterns across the performance space

Choreographic Tools: Devices such as repetition, contrast, canon, unison, variation, formation changes, mirroring, and the use of props

Practice and Refinement: Rehearsing, editing, and polishing movement sequences to create clarity, synchronisation, and expressive impact

Dance Choreography

Movement patterns in choreography

There are infinite ways in which patterns can be created through movement. The process of creating movement patterns can include:

linking movements into sequences;

designing shapes with the body;

arranging dancers into formations; and

creating symmetry or asymmetry.

Movement patterns in choreography carry layered meanings, especially within folk traditions. Rooted in everyday life and collective experience, these patterns emerge through simple steps, repetition, and group formations. In Indian regional dances, such movement vocabulary becomes a reflection of community life like shared work, social bonds, and collective celebration expressed through bodies moving together in rhythm.

Human formations, such as pyramids, are commonly seen in several Indian dance forms.

Nava Nritya of Manjushree Chaki Sarkar

Manjushree Chaki Sarkar was trained in Indian dance forms in Kolkata in the 1950s. She later spent several years in the USA, where she was exposed to Western modern and contemporary dance. On returning to India, she felt the need to develop a new contemporary dance vocabulary rooted in Indian aesthetics. Drawing on her background in cultural anthropology, she reimagined her work as Nava Nritya, creating a new dance vocabulary based on Indian aesthetics. She was assisted in this work by her daughter Ranjabati.

Activity 13.2

Creating your choreography

Divide yourself into four groups. Choose your lead choregrapher.

Fig. 13.12. Contemporary Kathak

choreography

First, each choreographer creates a solo movement portraying a concept of their choice (for example, contemporary theme, or depiction of nature like rain and thunderstorms, or celebration of festivals, etc.). They may choose any movement vocabulary they are comfortable with. Next, each lead choreographer must adapt their solo phrase into a group choreography, exploring formations, spacing, timing, and interaction. The choreography can include aspects of all four abhinayas, such as:

expressive, stylised movements (use of hand gestures, animation, and mime);

rhythmic syllables and spoken word;

props; and

emotional expression.

Each solo choreographer presents their choreography, followed by the group performance of the same.

Observe each group’s performance and explore how it can change and how expression changes with group interaction and usage of space in a:

solo performance; and

group performance

Note to the teacher: The number of lead choreographers, grouping, presentation mode, and occasion, etc., may be decided based on the teacher’s or group’s discretion. Encourage students to perform their work in class or at a school event.
