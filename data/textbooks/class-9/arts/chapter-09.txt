Music and Migration

Migration is the movement of people from one place to another to settle in a new location. When populations such as people of a country or an ethnic group that originated from the same place settle in a new country, they form what is called a diaspora.

Over thousands of years, people moved in and out of the Indian subcontinent. And, with them, they carried not just physical possessions, but also their music. The music they carried served many purposes — it provided links to their home, helped them bond with fellow migrants, preserved their cultural practices, and also allowed them to express their feelings and hardships. Their music blended with local sounds, creating new, hybrid genres, and enriched the cultural landscape.

Chutney music

It emerged from the migration of Indians from Bihar and Uttar Pradesh to the Caribbean islands in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Over time, Bhojpuri folk songs, bhajans, and instruments like the dholak blended with Caribbean styles such as soca and calypso. This fusion created a lively hybrid genre. Its lyrics mix Bhojpuri, Hindi, English, and Creole, reflecting everyday life and cultural identity. Instruments like the dholak, tassa, dhantal, and keyboards give Chutney its energetic, dance-driven sound.

Bhangra in the United Kingdom

A vibrant folk music and dance tradition from Punjab, Bhangra is rooted in energetic dhol rhythms and celebration. When Punjabi migrants settled in the UK between the 1950s and 1980s, they brought their music with them as a way to preserve their identity and community. Growing up between Punjabi homes and British cities, young musicians blended Bhangra with reggae, hip-hop, pop, and electronic sounds. This fusion led to the emergence of UK Bhangra, which is a blend of fast beats, heavy bass, Punjabi lyrics, and modern instruments.

Families of Musical Instruments

We have studied the classification of instruments into four categories, namely string (tata), wind (suṣhira), percussion (avanaddha), and solid (ghana), according to the Nāṭyaśhāstra. What is most remarkable is that this classification applies to musical instruments found anywhere in the world. Musical instruments may also be classified in other ways such as based on the musical genre they belong to, which country or region they come from, or similar shared characteristics.

The lute family

129

It includes plucked string instruments found across Asia and Europe. All of these share a basic design—a wide resonating body and a narrow neck—but differ in materials and construction. The resonating top, or belly, may be made of wood, as in the West Asian oud, or skin, as in the banjo. Some instruments use highly specific materials — the Japanese shamisen traditionally uses cat skin (skin of dead animals), the Puerto Rican cuatro uses seasoned jagrumo wood, and the Indian sarod has a stretched goatskin top. In recent times, synthetic materials have also been adopted.

The flute family

The flute is among the most ancient types of musical instruments. Archaeologists have uncovered flutes dating back tens of thousands of years. At its very basic level, a flute is a hollow tube that produces sound when the player blows air into it or across a hole on its surface. Found in nearly every culture, flutes reveal how different societies shaped the same basic idea of astonishing variety into instruments. The veena is one of the oldest string instruments of India. It is traditionally crafted from a single block of wood, most commonly jackwood. The instrument has two principal parts — a resonator called the kuḍam and a long hollow neck called the daṇḍi. At the end of the daṇḍi is a decorative carving of the yazhi, a mythical creature. The veena is central to Carnatic Music and is most commonly played in southern India.
