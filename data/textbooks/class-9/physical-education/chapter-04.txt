This unit emphasises the importance of making sports accessible and inclusive for everyone, regardless of age, gender, varied ability, or socio-economic background. It highlights the concept of sports as a fundamental human right, as outlined in global frameworks like the UNESCO Charter of Physical Education and the European Sports Charter. This unit explores how sports can promote happiness, unity, and good health, while fostering social inclusion and breaking barriers. It also highlights the challenges faced by marginalised groups, such as a lack of access to facilities, financial constraints, societal attitudes, and provides measures to promote inclusiveness. These include developing accessible infrastructure, enforcing anti-discrimination policies, promoting grassroots programmes, and supporting traditional and indigenous games like Kabaddi and Kho-Kho. Through activities and discussions, students are encouraged to design inclusive games, explore barriers to participation, and propose solutions to make sports more accessible. The unit emphasises the transformative power of sports in building teamwork, self-esteem, and community bonds, while fostering peace, equality, and mutual respect. It inspires students to advocate for inclusiveness and work towards creating a society where sports are truly for all.

Inclusiveness of Sports

Inclusiveness in sports refers to the principle and practice of ensuring that everyone, regardless of their background, abilities, gender, age, or social status, has the opportunity to participate and benefit from sporting activities.

Key Dimensions of Inclusiveness

Inclusiveness is not restricted to one-size-fits-all. It operates across multiple dimensions:

1. Gender Inclusiveness: Ensuring equal opportunities for both women and men in sports participation, coaching, and leadership. Initiatives like ‘This Girl Can’ promote female involvement and visibility in sports. Games such as Korfball, Ultimate Frisbee, and Mixed Doubles in various sports are examples that encourage equal participation.

99 Sports and Inclusivity

2. Disability Inclusion: Adapted sports enable persons with disabilities to participate fully. The Paralympic Games and Special Olympics are prime examples of platforms that celebrate athletic ability regardless of physical or intellectual challenges.

3. Socio-economic Inclusion: Removing cost barriers by providing free or affordable access to facilities, equipment, and coaching ensures that everyone, regardless of their economic background, has the opportunity to participate.

4. Cultural and Ethnic Inclusion: Incorporating ethnosports (traditional and indigenous games) alongside mainstream sports promotes cultural identity and attracts more diverse participants. For instance, including sports like Kabaddi and Kho-kho in India ensures local relevance and different government training centres.

5. Age Inclusion: Sports programmes must cater to all age groups, from children to older adults. Modified rules, age-friendly equipment, and non-competitive formats help maintain lifelong participation.

Measures to Promote Inclusiveness in Sports

Develop accessible sports infrastructure for all

It is important to construct accessible parks, playgrounds, and stadiums so that everyone, including children, the elderly, and those with disabilities, may use them. For example, gender-neutral restrooms, tactile walkways for the blind, wheelchair-accessible seating, and ramps.

Enforce equal opportunity and anti-discrimination policies

It must be specifically stated in the regulations that anyone can participate in sports, regardless of gender, disability, caste, religion, or socio-economic status. Strict anti-discrimination laws encourage fairness and respect.

Promote grassroots and community-level sports programmes

Local or community-based sporting events may be planned where women, children, and excluded communities can participate without worrying about being judged. Street sports events, school- level competitions, and community tournaments all help to make play more inclusive and accessible.

Provide inclusive training for coaches and officials

Sports officials, coaches, and referees play an essential role in creating a fair environment. To prevent bias, they should be trained in inclusive practices, such as assuring equal playing time, modifying rules for athletes with disabilities, and using positive language.

Run awareness and media campaigns promoting diversity

Featuring women athletes, para-athletes, and persons from various cultural backgrounds through posters, social media posts, and community announcements encourages society to respect inclusivity.

Support traditional and indigenous games

Encouraging indigenous games promotes cultural inclusion by treating regional customs with same respect. For example, regional sports like kabaddi, kho-kho, or wrestling allows participation by individuals from a variety of social and economic backgrounds without the need for costly equipment.

Financial challenge is a major obstacle to inclusion. Sports and Disability

In a small village in India lived a boy who was full of energy and loved playing with other children. One day, he suffered from a serious illness that left him with a disability. Walking and moving became difficult for him.

Many people in his village felt sorry for him and thought his life would be full of struggles. But he was determined not to give up. He believed that he could still do something special. He found hope in swimming. Even though his body was weak, but his spirit was strong. He started practising in the village well and later in proper swimming pools.

With years of hard work, he became a skilled swimmer. His courage and dedication helped him to participate in the Paralympics, where he represented India with pride.

His story shows us that disability is not inability. What matters is willpower, confidence, and never giving up.

Sports are not just about winning medals, but about enjoying movement, building health, and learning teamwork. Sports have a special way of bringing people together. When we play a game, we share movement, laughter, effort, and teamwork. In those moments, differences matter less, and what matters more is participation and enjoyment. That is why sports are often seen as a space where everyone can feel included. For people with disabilities, sports can be especially empowering, helping them develop independence, self-belief, and a strong sense of belonging.

In the past, many people with disabilities were left out of games and physical activities because others did not understand how to include them. Today, attitudes are changing,

and we understand that disability does not mean inability. With some changes in rules, equipment, or the playing environment, everyone can take part in sports. These are called adapted sports, and they show how sports can be made welcoming for all.

Disability is defined as a condition that restricts a person’s ability to perform everyday activities due to impairments in physical, sensory, intellectual, or psychological functioning. Disabilities can be congenital (present at birth) or acquired later in life due to illness, accident, or ageing.

The World Health Organisation (WHO) and the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 (India) classify disabilities into broad categories: physical, sensory, intellectual, learning, psychosocial, and multiple disabilities.

To ensure inclusion and equal opportunities, the Government of India officially recognises certain conditions and offers benefits and assistance. Under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, twenty-one types of disabilities are recognised for support. Such measures remind us that every person deserves respect, opportunities, and the chance to participate fully — including in sports.

Barriers to Adaptive Sports Participation

People with disabilities often face many challenges in taking part in sports. Some common barriers include lack of accessible playgrounds, high cost of equipment, and difficulty in transportation. Many also struggle with limited knowledge of opportunities, shortage of activities, or lack of social support. Health issues, pain, and risk of injury can also hinder participation. These barriers show why schools and communities must work together to make sports inclusive for everyone.

The Meaning of “Paralympic”

TRIVIA

At present, the Paralympic Games include 28 sports — 22 summer sports and 6 winter sports. These sports are adapted to allow athletes with different types of disabilities to compete at the highest level.

The word Paralympic comes from the Greek word para (meaning “beside” or “alongside”) and the word olympic. This illustrates that the Paralympic Games are held parallel to the Olympic Games, giving athletes with disabilities the same chance to compete at an international level.

The Paralympic Games

The Paralympic Games are the highest level of competition for elite athletes with physical disabilities. Just like Olympic athletes, Paralympians train hard, follow strict qualification rules, and compete with the goal of achieving their personal best — and a gold medal. The Paralympics give athletes with disabilities an environment of excellence, fairness, and pride.

Now that we’ve thought about Rohan's situation, let’s understand the meaning of etiquette and how it applies to everyone.

Etiquette means good manners and the right way of behaving with others. It helps us to be polite, respectful, and friendly in our daily life. General etiquette includes simple things like saying “thank you”, listening carefully when someone talks, and respecting other people’s space.

Disability etiquettes are about the correct ways of behaving with people who have disabilities. It teaches us to treat them with respect and kindness, without pity or overprotecting them. People with disabilities want to be seen as individuals first, not just for their disability. By following disability etiquette, we can make them feel included, confident, and equal in society.

What are General Etiquettes?

These are basic principles of respectful, courteous behaviour toward all individuals:

1. Be Respectful: Treat everyone with dignity and consideration.

2. Be Inclusive: Don’t assume people can or can’t do things based on appearance.

3. Ask Before Helping: Never assume someone needs help— always ask first.

4. Use First-Person (when preferred): One example would be “person with a disability” instead of “disabled person” (though some prefer identity-first, like “autistic person”—so follow individual preferences).

5. Don’t Stare, Point, or Make Jokes: Avoid drawing attention to someone’s disability or differences.

Disability Etiquettes

Respectful interaction with people who have disabilities includes awareness, sensitivity, and using appropriate language and behaviour.

When interacting with people who have different types of disabilities, here are some specific guidelines to follow:

1. Wheelchair Users

• Don’t push someone’s wheelchair without permission.

• Speak directly to the person, not to someone with them.

STEP Model – For Inclusive Learning

Understanding etiquette is important, but how do we actually make our classrooms and activities inclusive? The STEP Model gives us a practical way to adapt any activity so everyone can participate.

The STEP Model is used to make activities inclusive and suitable for everyone, including persons with disabilities.

It focuses on changing four things: Space, Task, Equipment, and People.

S = Space

• Meaning: Adjust the environment or place where the activity is done.

• Examples

– Make the play area smaller for easier movement.

– Use a flat, safe surface instead of uneven ground.

– Reduce noise and distractions in the environment.

T = Task

• Meaning: Change the way the activity or task is done.

• Examples

– Simplify the rules of the game.

– Break a big activity into smaller steps.

– Allow extra time for completing the task.

E = Equipment

• Meaning: Use or modify equipment so everyone can take part.

• Examples

– Use bigger and softer balls.

– Use bright-coloured or sound-enabled objects for easy visibility/hearing.

– Provide lightweight equipment for easier handling

P = People

• Meaning: Change how people are grouped or paired in an activity.

• Examples

– Pair a strong student with one who needs more support.

– Make smaller groups so everyone gets a chance.

Importance of STEP Model (Single-Line Points)

1. Promotes Inclusion and Equality: It allows all students, with or without disabilities, to participate together.

2. Builds Confidence: Adapted activities help disabled students feel capable and proud.

3. Encourages Participation: It motivates students with disabilities to join games and classroom tasks.

4. Develops Empathy and Teamwork: It teaches students to support, respect, and work with each other.

Young Athlete

Swami Vivekananda once said, “You will be nearer to heaven through football than through the study of the Gītā.”

By this, he did not mean that reading the Bhagavad Gītā was unimportant. He wanted to teach young people that physical strength and good health are just as important as spiritual learning. Vivekananda encouraged the youth to play games, exercise, and stay fit, because physical development is the first step toward becoming confident, disciplined, and spiritually aware.

Youth Participation and Health in Sports

Many children and teenagers today participate in different sports, and participation is growing every year. Young athletes often train throughout the year and may focus on one particular sport. By the time they reach adolescence, some may even compete at national or international levels.

Preteens often dedicate numerous hours weekly to sports such as tennis, gymnastics, hockey, or football. They sometimes travel with their teams to compete against peers of similar skill levels. Engaging in sports from a young age offers multiple health advantages, including enhanced fitness, teamwork, and confidence.

However, intense training and competition can sometimes lead to injuries or health problems. Conditions like asthma, heart defects, or Type 1 diabetes may appear or be diagnosed for the first time when a child starts regular sports training. Because young bodies are still growing, it is important to:

• monitor health carefully,

• ensure proper warm-up and rest,

• prevent and manage injuries, and

• encourage balanced nutrition and hydration.

With the right care and guidance, young athletes can enjoy sports safely, stay healthy, and perform at their best.

Growth, Performance, and Fair Play in Youth Sports

• Rapid Changes During Growth: During childhood and adolescence, the human body changes more than at any other time in life. As children grow, their height, weight, strength, and coordination develop at different speeds. These changes affect how well they perform in sports.

• Peak Performance and Maturity: Some famous athletes, like Simone Biles (gymnastics champion at 16) and Alina Zagitova (Olympic gold medalist at 15), reached great success in their teenage years. However, for most people, sports performance improves gradually through childhood and adolescence and reaches its peak in their early 20s.

In younger children (under 12), early and late physical maturity doesn’t make a big difference. But during adolescence, those who mature earlier often have an advantage in sports like football, hockey, and rugby, because they are usually stronger and faster than late-maturing players of the same age.

Aerobic and Anaerobic Capacity

• Aerobic Capacity means how well your heart and lungs work to supply oxygen during activity. It increases with growth and training.

• Anaerobic Capacity is the ability to use short bursts of power and energy, like sprinting or jumping. It also improves as the muscles grow stronger with age.

In boys and girls before puberty, strength levels are quite similar. During and after puberty, boys gain more muscle mass, which increases their overall strength and power.

Creating a Level Playing Field

Have you ever noticed that students of the same age can look very different in height, strength, and build? This is because everyone grows and develops at their own pace. In sports, this can sometimes create unequal competition. For example, one 13-year-old who matures early may be much taller and stronger than another 13-year- old who is still growing. When such players compete against each other, it can be unfair and may even increase the risk of injury.

To make competitions safer and fairer, some sports organise players by weight categories rather than by age alone. This is common in games like rugby and combat sports. Grouping athletes this way helps ensure that participants compete against others of similar size and strength, giving everyone a fair chance to compete and enjoy the game.

Early Specialisation: Good or Bad?

The idea of early specialisation—when a child focuses on just one sport from a very young age—has been debated for many years. Some coaches and teams believe that the earlier a child starts training seriously in one sport, the better chance they have of becoming a star athlete, like Lionel Messi in football or LeBron James in basketball.

However, many experts warn that early specialisation can have serious drawbacks. Training too much at too early can lead to injuries, stress, and burnout. It can also take away the joy of playing and reduce time for free play, rest, and learning other skills.

Recommendations

• Play multiple sports: Children should be encouraged to try different sports before choosing the one that they enjoy the most.

• Include free play: Unstructured play, like running, climbing, or casual games, helps build coordination and motor skills naturally.

• Balance training: Young athletes should not train more hours per week than their age (for example, a 12-year-old shouldn’t train more than 12 hours a week).

• Avoid overtraining: Too much intense practice can cause injuries and mental exhaustion.

• Build strength safely: All young people can benefit from general fitness and strength-building exercises done under supervision.

• Stay safe: Coaches and parents must ensure young athletes are protected from any form of pressure, bullying, or abuse.

Risks of Early Specialisation

Early specialisation can lead to:

• Overuse injuries (because of repeating the same movements).

• Burnout or loss of interest.

• Social isolation and less time with friends.

• Overdependence on coaches or parents.

• Stunted growth or delayed development.

Understanding Menstruation

Menstruation (often called a “period”) is the monthly shedding of the uterine lining through the vagina.

• Periods typically last 3 to 7 days, though more commonly 3 to 5 days.

• A normal menstrual cycle occurs every 21 to 35 days.

• Women typically have periods until the age of 45–55 years, after which they experience menopause, meaning the natural end of menstruation and fertility.

Menstrual health is an important part of overall well-being. Regular cycles are a sign of a healthy reproductive system.

Menstrual Dysfunction

Sometimes, women and young girls experience irregularities or problems in their menstrual cycle. These are called menstrual dysfunctions.

A normal menstrual cycle happens once every 21–35 days. If it occurs more often, less often, or causes extreme pain or bleeding, it may indicate a problem.

Common Causes

• Overweight or underweight

• Stress and anxiety

• Poor diet or eating disorders

• Sudden changes in physical activity or training schedules

• Hormonal imbalance or certain illnesses

Types of Menstrual Disorders

• Emotional and physical symptoms before periods such as mood swings, headaches, tiredness, and irritability.

The Female Athlete Triad

While sports bring many health benefits for girls and women, some athletes face health issues due to imbalanced diet and training.

One such condition is called the Female Athlete Triad, first described by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) in 1992.

The triad includes three interrelated conditions:

1. Low Energy Availability (with or without eating disorder)

2. Menstrual Dysfunction (absence or irregularity of periods)

3. Low Bone Mineral Density (weak bones)

These three are connected and often occur together in athletes who train intensely but don’t eat enough to meet their body’s energy needs.

Low Energy Availability

This happens when athletes do not eat enough food to balance the energy they spend during exercise.

• It may occur due to dieting, fear of weight gain, or eating disorders such as:

– Anorexia Nervosa: An extreme fear of gaining weight, leading to eating very little or over-exercising.

– Bulimia Nervosa: Eating large amounts of food and then using unhealthy methods to remove calories, such as vomiting or using laxatives.

• Common symptoms include fatigue, weight loss, stress fractures, irregular periods, anxiety, and weakness.

Proper nutrition and professional guidance can prevent or correct this condition.

Low Bone Mineral Density (BMD)

Bone health depends on proper nutrition, hormones, and exercise. When women have low oestrogen levels or poor diet, bones become fragile and weak, a condition known as osteoporosis.

• This can cause stress fractures, pain, or deformity later in life.

• Bone growth and strengthening happen mainly during childhood and adolescence, so maintaining proper diet and physical activity during these years is vital.

Age is Just a Number

Fauja Singh was a British Sikh marathon runner of Indian origin and an inspiration for people of all ages. He was born on April 1, 1911, in Beas Pind village, Jalandhar, Punjab. During his childhood, he was weak and could not walk properly until the age of five. He worked as a farmer for most of his early life.

After the death of his wife and son, Fauja Singh moved to England and took up running to overcome his grief. At the age of 89, he started training seriously and completed his first marathon in 2000 at the London Marathon. He went on to run marathons in London, Toronto, and New York.

In 2011, at the age of 100, Fauja Singh became the first centenarian to complete a full marathon at the Toronto Waterfront Marathon. He also set multiple world age-group records in track events. Known as the “Turbaned Tornado”, he promoted fitness culture through sports.

Fauja Singh retired from competitive running at the age of 101. His life proves that age is not a barrier to physical fitness and that regular exercise promotes both physical and mental well-being.

Have you noticed that many people today stay active and energetic even as they grow older? In recent years, the average age of participants in endurance sports such as long-distance running, cycling, and swimming has increased. Many older athletes train regularly, follow healthy routines, and set personal goals to keep themselves motivated. Their example shows that with the right attitude and care, the human body can remain strong and active for decades. It reminds us that staying active keeps both the body strong and the mind fresh—age may bring changes, but it does not have to limit our abilities or dreams.

As people grow older, the body naturally changes. Muscle strength, balance, and energy levels may gradually decrease, and some people become less active. This can increase the risk of health

problems such as heart disease, diabetes, obesity, and weak bones. However, regular physical activity can slow down these changes and help older adults live longer, healthier, and more independent lives. That is why exercise is often called “the best medicine for ageing”.

Staying active in later years does not always mean playing competitive sports. It can include simple and enjoyable activities like walking, swimming, yoga, cycling, gardening, or light exercises at home. The key is to keep moving regularly and choose activities that feel enjoyable and safe.

Healthy ageing means living an active, independent, and meaningful life as we grow older. It involves maintaining physical fitness, mental alertness, and emotional well-being so that people can continue doing the things they value — such as travelling, meeting friends, or playing with grandchildren. This depends not only on a person’s own health and habits but also on having a supportive environment that encourages activity and social connection.

Safety and Precautions

Older adults should always consult a doctor before starting a new exercise programme, especially if they have heart or joint problems. Some safety tips include: 
• Start slowly and increase the intensity and frequency of the activity gradually. 
• Warm up and cool down properly. 
• Stay hydrated and wear comfortable shoes. 
• Avoid very heavy or high-impact exercises. 
• Exercise regularly, not occasionally. 
• Doing light activity consistently brings great benefits.
